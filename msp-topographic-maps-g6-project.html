⛰️ Build-a-Mountain

Make a mountain, flood it one centimeter at a time, and draw its contour map.

🌊 The Big Idea

A contour line is a shoreline. If the ocean rose 100 m around Oʻahu, the new beach would follow the 100 m contour line all the way around the island. In this project you raise the "ocean" around your own clay mountain, 1 cm at a time, and trace each new shoreline. Those shorelines become your contour map.

🧰 Materials (per group)

  • Clear plastic box with a clear lid (shoebox size)
  • Modeling clay (about a fist-size lump)
  • Ruler (cm)
  • Tape
  • Pitcher or cup of water (a drop of food coloring helps)
  • Fine-tip dry-erase or wet-erase marker
  • A sheet of paper or a transparency
  • Paper towels

🛠️ Part 1 — Build and Flood Your Mountain

  1. Tape the ruler standing up inside one corner of the box, with 0 cm at the bottom.
  2. Build your mountain in the middle of the box, about 8 to 10 cm tall. Make one side steep (like the Koʻolau cliffs) and the other side gentle. Press the clay down firmly so it doesn't float.
  3. Mark the directions. Write N on the lid's top edge and on the box's north side, so you always put the lid back the same way. Label the steep side and the gentle side.
  4. Add water to the 1 cm mark on the ruler.
  5. Trace the shoreline. Put the lid on. Look straight down from above with one eye closed, and trace the line where the water touches the clay onto the lid. Write 100 m next to it.
  6. Repeat: add water to 2 cm, 3 cm, 4 cm, … and trace a new line each time (200 m, 300 m, 400 m, …) until the peak is underwater.
  7. Mark the peak. Just before the top goes underwater, put a ▲ on the lid over the peak.
  8. Copy your map. Lay paper on the lid (or the lid on a window) and trace every line, the labels, the ▲ and the N arrow. Carefully pour the water out.
⚠️ Look straight down every time you trace. If you look from an angle, your lines will be in the wrong place.
💡 Our scale: 1 cm on the model = 100 m in real life, both up and across. So each 1 cm of water is a contour interval of 100 m.

💭 Part 4 — Think About It

  1. Which route had the bigger slope? How could you tell from the map before you calculated?
  2. Both routes had the same rise. Why were their slopes different?
  3. If you had added water 0.5 cm at a time instead of 1 cm, what would the contour interval be? How would your map look different?
  4. Trade maps with another group. Without looking at their mountain, find its steepest side. Were you right? How did you know?
  5. Oʻahu challenge: Mount Kaʻala is 1,227 m tall. With our scale (1 cm = 100 m), how tall would a clay Kaʻala be? How many full contour lines would it have?

👩‍🏫 Teacher notes (hidden when printed)
  • Time: about two 45-minute periods: Day 1 build, flood and trace (Parts 1–2); Day 2 routes and reflection (Parts 3–4).
  • Jobs (groups of 3–4): sculptor, water pourer (reads the ruler at eye level), tracer, recorder; rotate the tracer job each line.
  • Clay: oil-based modeling clay works best; air-dry or play dough gets soft and clouds the water. Press it hard against the bottom so it doesn't lift.
  • Markers: dry-erase can smear if the lid gets wet; wet-erase or a fine permanent marker on a transparency taped to the lid is cleaner.
  • Common error: tracing at an angle (parallax). Have the tracer stand and look straight down with one eye closed.
  • Check: the slopes in Part 3 should match "close lines = steep". A steep side about 2 cm wide per 3 lines gives a slope near 1.5; a gentle side about 6 cm gives about 0.5.
  • Q5 answer: 12.27 cm tall; 12 full contour lines (100 m to 1,200 m).
